# One Function for Every Prime

## The uniformity of p-adic splitting densities, explained

**Claude Fable 5** (an AI system built by Anthropic). *The mathematics presented here builds on and extends the local Chebotarev density theorem of Asvin G, Yifan Wei, and John Yin (arXiv:2212.00294). The formalization it describes was carried out in the Lean proof assistant by the author, working as the orchestrating agent of a large collaboration of automated provers and auditors, under the direction of Asvin G. Sources, the formal statements, and an expandable map of the whole development accompany this document.*

### Abstract

Choose a prime, and choose at random a polynomial of some fixed degree, with leading coefficient one, whose other coefficients are integers of the p-adic world attached to that prime. The polynomial breaks into irreducible factors, and the list of factor degrees together with their ramification — a notion defined in the first section — is called the polynomial's splitting type. Each splitting type occurs with a definite probability, called its density. This document explains a body of results, formalized and checked in the Lean proof assistant, about how these densities depend on the prime. The centerpiece for quadratic polynomials is unconditional: for each of the three quadratic splitting types there is a single rational function which, evaluated at the size of the prime, gives the density at every prime without exception — including the prime two, where quadratic ramification is wild and the classical methods fail. For higher degrees the same architecture is developed in full: a classification tree, a finite catalogue of local patterns with an explicit bound at every degree, and a linear-algebra procedure that manufactures one candidate rational function per splitting type; a machine-checked theorem then derives the density statement at any prime satisfying an explicitly stated regularity condition, from a displayed list of hypotheses. Which parts of that list are proved, which are verified by exhaustive computation, and which remain conjectures is stated precisely in the middle of this document, before the proof is described.

## 1. The question

The p-adic integers attached to a prime form a number system in which two integers count as close when their difference is divisible by a high power of that prime. Every ordinary integer belongs to this system, and a general member is an infinite expansion in powers of the prime — a base-two integer with infinitely many binary digits, say, when the prime is two. Just as a decimal expansion is generated digit by digit, a random p-adic integer is generated by choosing each digit independently and uniformly. This digit-by-digit recipe defines the uniform probability measure on the p-adic integers (the normalized Haar measure, in standard terminology), and it is the only notion of randomness used in this document. A random polynomial of a fixed degree means: leading coefficient one, every other coefficient drawn independently at random by this recipe.

Over the fraction field of the p-adic integers, every such polynomial factors into irreducible pieces, uniquely up to reordering. Two integers describe each piece. The first is its degree. The second is its ramification index, which measures how the piece interacts with the prime itself, and can be read off a simple example. A root of the polynomial "the square of the unknown minus the prime" is a square root of the prime: adjoining it creates a number whose square has a single power of the prime in it, so powers of the new number slice the prime into finer gradations — two gradations per power of the prime, in this case. The ramification index of an irreducible piece is the number of such gradations its roots create; it is always a divisor-friendly companion of the degree, at least one and at most the degree. A piece with ramification index one is called unramified.

The splitting type of a polynomial is the collection of these pairs — degree and ramification index, one pair for each irreducible piece, with repetitions counted. For quadratic polynomials there are three splitting types: two pieces of degree one ("split"); one unramified piece of degree two ("inert"); one piece of degree two with ramification index two ("ramified"). Because a splitting type is an event in the coin-flipping sense — a measurable set of coefficient sequences — it has a probability, and that probability is its density at the given prime.

The question: how does the density of a fixed splitting type vary with the prime?

## 2. The answer, and exactly how much of it is proved

The classical answer, described in the next section, covers all but finitely many primes at each degree. The results described here concern all primes at once, and they come in two strengths.

**The quadratic theorem (unconditional).** For polynomials of degree two, each of the three splitting types has its density given at every prime by a single rational function of the size of the residue field — the finite field of remainders modulo the prime. The ramified type has density one divided by one more than that size; the split and inert types share the remaining probability equally. At the prime two, where ramification of quadratics is wild in the sense of the next section, all three densities therefore equal one third — a value confirmed independently by exhaustive machine count over truncated coefficients. This theorem is machine-checked with no hypotheses beyond the logical axioms of the Lean proof assistant: the classification of quadratic polynomials, the vanishing of the undecided remainder, and the density values themselves are all certified, at every prime.

**The general architecture (conditional, with a certified skeleton).** For each higher degree, the development constructs: the full classification tree of polynomials at any prime; a finite catalogue of the local patterns the tree can exhibit, with an explicit bound depending only on the degree, and an exact machine-enumerated count of fifty-three patterns at degree three; and the linear-algebra procedure that attaches to each splitting type one candidate rational function. A capstone theorem, machine-checked from the logical axioms alone, then states: at any prime satisfying a regularity condition — two explicitly typed requirements on how that prime's counting data is organized — the density of each splitting type exists and equals the candidate function evaluated at the prime, the candidate functions sum to one identically, and the same value is reached by three separately certified readings of "density". Every assumption of this capstone is a named, displayed hypothesis in its formal statement, and its dependence classifies as follows.

- *Proved outright*: the two-sided counting bracket that squeezes the density, the convergence argument, the passage between the three density readings, the catalogue bound at every degree, the exact catalogue and the complete rational-function tables at degree three, and the supporting corpus of roughly seven hundred machine-checked statements.
- *Imported from the literature as audited citations, on a separate track*: exactly two results — the theorem of the index of Guàrdia, Montes, and Nart (arXiv:0807.2620), in a deliberately narrowed form adopted after two broader formulations were refuted by explicit counterexamples; and the cell-decomposition recursion of Denef and Igusa, used only in an independent measure-theoretic route and not in the capstone. The capstone's certificate lists no axioms beyond the three built into Lean's logic.
- *Verified by exhaustive computation, not yet proved*: a small list of combinatorial kernels at the base of the carry analysis. Their statements for all parameters at once are conjectures; their instances have been checked exactly over their full stated ranges — in one case all forty thousand three hundred seventy-eight boundary configurations, in another all one hundred forty pool cases — and cross-checked against an independent p-adic factorization oracle.
- *Open, and stated in the formal text as such*: the connection of the capstone's per-prime hypothesis bundle to the classifier itself at every degree beyond the certified cases. Building these instances is the main remaining work, and thirteen of the hypothesis slots are placeholders whose supporting vocabulary is not yet constructed — at present they impose no real requirement, which the formal statement's own documentation says in as many words. Nine fenced unproved statements remain in the development, each named and each with a written record of exactly what it asserts.

The reader now holds the whole truth-status map. Everything after this point is explanation: what the objects are, why wild primes broke the classical arguments, and how the proof works.

## 3. The classical picture, and where it stops

At any fixed degree, all but finitely many primes are tame: the prime does not divide any of the ramification indices in play. At tame primes the densities have been understood for some time. The reduction of the polynomial modulo the prime determines most of its factorization, by Hensel's classical lifting lemma; counting reductions over the residue field produces polynomial formulas in the field's size; and tame ramification contributes explicit correction weights. The outcome is that each density is visibly the value of a fixed rational function of the residue-field size. This is the local expression of the Chebotarev circle of ideas, and in the form used here — densities of splitting behavior along any finite map between p-adic spaces — it is the theorem of the 2022 paper of Asvin G, Yifan Wei, and John Yin.

The finitely many excluded primes at each degree are the wild ones: those dividing at least one available ramification index. Squaring doubles; so degree two is wild exactly at the prime two, and degree six has wild behavior at two and three. At a wild prime the tools above genuinely fail rather than merely strain. Hensel's lemma cannot separate factors whose reductions coincide, and at wild primes the interesting polynomials are precisely those with badly repeating reductions. The geometric route that proves the tame theorem also breaks for a structural reason: it passes through a resolution of singularities equivariant under the symmetric group on the roots, and in residue characteristic dividing the factorial of the degree that group is not linearly reductive, so the construction is unavailable.

What replaces clean lifting at a wild prime is digit arithmetic. Deciding a wild factorization forces one to track how carries — the overflow from one digit position of a coefficient into the next — propagate through the successive approximation steps. The dependence of the answer on the polynomial becomes a delicate condition on finitely many digits, at unbounded depth. Densities at wild primes were accordingly computed case by case — degree by degree, prime by prime — and the published values, while perfectly explicit, carried no visible common shape with the tame formulas.

## 4. The uniformity theorem

The discovery is that a common shape exists and is total: the rational function is one and the same across every prime.

For quadratics this is the unconditional theorem stated in the scope ledger above. For a general degree, the certified statement reads: for each splitting type of that degree there is one rational function such that at every prime passing the regularity condition, the density of the type exists and equals that function's value at the residue-field size — with the undecided remainder provably vanishing, the candidate functions summing to one, and the equality holding simultaneously for the classifier's own reading of the density, for the true splitting-type reading, and for a direct reading over the p-adic integers. The regularity condition holds in every case that has been decided; that it holds at every prime and every degree is one of the three conjectures in the final section.

The values themselves illustrate what uniformity means. The quadratic ramified density — one divided by one more than the residue-field size — is a formula a tame computation produces easily at odd primes. The theorem says the prime two obeys the same formula, even though every step of the tame computation is invalid there. The wild answer was never a new answer; it was the old answer, reached by a longer road.

## 5. The tree of successive approximations

The proof abandons direct factorization in favor of the algorithm that decides factorization. In a line of development running from Ore's work in the 1920s through Okutsu's invariants to the types of Montes and collaborators, an irreducible polynomial over the p-adic integers is described by a tower of approximations. The ground level records an irreducible factor of the polynomial's reduction over the residue field. Each subsequent level refines the current approximation by two data: a slope, read off a Newton polygon — the lower convex hull of a plot of digit-depths of coefficients, whose segment slopes expose the fine structure of roots — and a new irreducible polynomial over an enlarged residue field, called the residual polynomial of that level, which governs how the approximation can be refined further. After finitely many levels the approximation determines the factor, and in particular its degree and ramification index.

Running this procedure on all polynomials simultaneously organizes them into a tree. A node of the tree is a partial classification — a stack of slopes and residual factors so far — and the polynomials passing through a node are exactly those whose digits, read to the depth the node consumes, satisfy the node's conditions. A given polynomial drives its factors down finitely many branches, and its splitting type is read off where its branches terminate. Randomness of the polynomial becomes randomness of the descent: the digits not yet consumed decide, with computable probabilities, which children of a node the descent visits. The formal development implements this picture in full: the tree, the descent, the reading of splitting types at the leaves, and — the technically decisive part — the exact identification of "the set of polynomials at this node" with an explicit finite union of digit-cylinder sets whose measure can be written down. All wild-prime difficulty is now localized in the transition probabilities from a node to its children, where the carries live.

## 6. A finite catalogue of patterns

An infinite tree with computable transitions is machinery, not yet a theorem. The theorem-grade discovery is a finiteness: the tree, at every prime of a given degree, is assembled from finitely many kinds of local pieces.

Inspect a node and ask what actually determines the shape of the subtree below it, together with all its transition probabilities. Not the node's whole history: only a bounded packet — the current slope data, the residual polynomial's combinatorial class, and a bounded record of the carry state. Call the equivalence class of this packet the pattern of the node (the development calls it a species). The count of possible patterns is bounded by an explicit expression in the degree alone; the bound is machine-checked at every degree. At degree three the catalogue is not merely bounded but enumerated: exactly fifty-three patterns occur, and the closure of the catalogue under all transitions — nothing outside the fifty-three is ever reached — is certified by kernel computation. The prime does not enter the catalogue; it enters only the transition probabilities, and there only through the size of the residue field, polynomially.

This finiteness converts the descent into a walk on a fixed finite directed graph, the same graph for every prime. Wild primes lose their exceptional status: wildness means the walk can traverse certain carry-sensitive edges — among them self-loops, where an approximation deepens without changing its pattern — and those edges simply carry their own rational transition weights. The carry analysis that justifies this sits in the longest chapters of the development: a carry algebra, a small quotient ring in which the propagation of digit overflow through one level of the tower is computed once and for all, and transport theorems moving weights and residues from each level to the next.

## 7. From walks to one rational function

Counting is now linear algebra. Collect, for each pattern, the probability that a descent begun at a node of that pattern realizes a prescribed remainder of the splitting type. These quantities satisfy one linear equation per pattern: the first step of the descent conditions on the child reached, and each child poses the same question one level deeper. The system is finite because the catalogue is; its coefficients are rational functions of the residue-field size because the transition weights are. Summing over all depths is a geometric series, and solving the system — inverting a matrix whose entries are the transition weights, with denominators controlled by an explicit determinant that is certified nonzero where the solve is invoked — expresses each density as a rational function of the residue-field size. This is the function the theorem attaches to the splitting type. At degree three the entire solve has been re-derived from the bottom up inside the formal development, and its output matches the independently recorded tables exactly, including all decided wild values at the two smallest primes, with the candidate functions summing to one at every level.

The remaining half of the argument is the squeeze. Reading only finitely many digits, the classifier decides most polynomials but not all; the undecided remainder must be shown to vanish in the limit. The development proves a two-sided bracket: at every reading depth, the count of decided polynomials of the given type lies between the candidate value diminished by an explicit envelope and the candidate value itself, both scaled to the reading depth. The envelope tends to zero — proved outright at degree two, and in an explicit threshold form at general degree, with the sharpest general form carried as one of the named open kernels. Together the bracket and the vanishing envelope force the density to exist and to equal the candidate value. That squeeze, with the identification clauses connecting the three readings of density, is precisely the machine-checked capstone.

## 8. What the machine checking contributes

A proof of this shape — an algorithm's tree, a catalogue with hundreds of cases, carry arithmetic at wild primes, a linear solve threaded through everything — is exactly the kind of argument where human review is least reliable: no single step is deep, and there are thousands of steps. Formalization contributes two things beyond checking them.

The first is a typed boundary. The capstone theorem's formal statement carries every assumption as a named hypothesis, so the boundary between proved and assumed is not prose but type-checked text; the classification in the scope ledger of section 2 is read off the statement itself, not reconstructed from a narrative. When part of the mathematics is open, the formal text says exactly which statement is missing and what it would provide.

The second is falsification pressure. During the campaign roughly twenty formal statements that compiled and looked plausible were refuted before acceptance by compiled countermodels — explicit witnesses, checked by the same kernel, that the statement as first written was false. Two proposed literature-citation axioms were caught overclaiming their sources and replaced by narrowed forms; one pair of statements was found to make the system inconsistent and was excised. Each incident is recorded in the development beside the corrected statement. The methodology — every batch of statements audited by an independent verifier in a fresh context before acceptance, with the audit's objections recorded — is part of what the published development documents.

## 9. Three conjectures

The certified results sit inside a program whose completion rests on three precisely stated conjectures.

**Exhaustiveness.** At degree two, the vanishing of the undecided remainder is proved at every prime. At higher degrees the vanishing is proved in threshold form under the capstone's hypotheses, and the unconditional general statement — the classification tree decides almost every polynomial, at every degree and prime — is a conjecture, verified computationally well beyond the certified range.

**Regularity at every prime.** The capstone's regularity condition — the requirement that a prime's counting data is organized as the linear solve expects — holds in every decided case. That it holds at every prime, for every degree, is a conjecture; its two clauses have been verified exactly across their full probed ranges.

**The functional equation.** The rational functions produced by the solve display a palindromic symmetry: substituting the reciprocal of the residue-field size transforms each function by a predictable factor. At tame primes this follows from a duality of Del Corso and Dvornicich. Its statement across all primes stands in the development as a named hypothesis that no certified result currently consumes; proving it is a conjecture of the same standing as the other two.

Beyond these, the development names its own next campaign: constructing, at each prime, the certified instance package that connects the classifier to the capstone's hypothesis bundle at every degree, as has been done in the decided cases. Each open item exists in the formal text as a typed statement whose proof would be checked by machine the day it is written.

# One Function for Every Prime

## A uniformity theorem for quadratic polynomials over the p-adic integers, and a machine-checked architecture for every degree

Written by Claude Fable 5, an AI system built by Anthropic, July 2026. The mathematics builds on the local Chebotarev density theorem of Asvin G, Yifan Wei, and John Yin (arXiv:2212.00294); the formalization described here was produced in the Lean proof assistant by the author, coordinating a large collection of automated proving and auditing runs, working with Asvin G. A companion page presents the formal development statement by statement, each beside its mathematical translation and certification status; references are collected at the end.

### Abstract

Fix a prime and consider polynomials with leading coefficient one whose remaining coefficients are p-adic integers chosen uniformly at random. Such a polynomial factors into irreducible pieces, and the collection of pieces has a splitting type: one pair of whole numbers per piece, recording how the piece interacts with the prime. Each splitting type is a measurable event and so has a probability, called its density at that prime. This document presents two results about these densities, both formalized in the Lean proof assistant, of deliberately different strengths. The first is a theorem with no unproved hypotheses: for quadratic polynomials, each of the three splitting types has one fixed rational function that yields its density at every prime — including the prime two, where quadratic ramification is wild and the classical computations do not apply. The second is a conditional theorem at every fixed degree: a machine-checked derivation showing that the density of each splitting type equals one fixed rational function of the prime, at any prime satisfying a stated regularity condition, from a stated list of hypotheses. Which hypotheses on that list are proved, which are conjectures supported by exhaustive finite computation, and which await construction is set out in a table in section 2, before any proof mechanism is described; the honest summary is that the quadratic theorem is finished, and the general-degree theorem is an architecture whose load-bearing conditions are precisely identified but not all discharged.

## 1. The question

The p-adic integers attached to a prime form a number system in which an integer is small when it is divisible by a high power of that prime. Every member has a digit expansion in powers of the prime — each digit drawn from the residues zero through one less than the prime — extending upward through ever higher powers; such expansions always converge in this number system, exactly as decimal expansions converge on the real line. Drawing each digit independently and uniformly defines the uniform probability measure on the p-adic integers; the finite-depth truncations of this recipe are consistent with one another, and the standard name for the resulting measure is the normalized Haar measure. A random polynomial of fixed degree means: leading coefficient one, each remaining coefficient drawn independently by this recipe. This is the only probability model in the paper; every "density" below is the measure of a set of coefficient sequences under it, and the sets in question are measurable because each is built from conditions on finitely many digits at a time, as section 5 makes explicit.

Enlarging the p-adic integers to allow division produces a field, and over that field every polynomial of the kind above factors into irreducible pieces, uniquely apart from reordering. Each piece determines an extension field, generated by a root, and the extension is measured by two whole numbers whose product is the degree of the piece. To define them, note that every nonzero p-adic number has a size, and the possible sizes form a ladder: the powers of one fixed ratio, one rung per power of the prime. In the extension generated by a root of an irreducible piece, the possible sizes again form a ladder, but its rungs may be finer: they are powers of a root of the original ratio, of some fixed whole-number order. That order is the ramification index of the piece. The second number, the residue degree, says how much the arithmetic of remainders grows: reducing the extension modulo the prime yields a finite field, and the residue degree is its dimension over the field of remainders modulo the prime. Ramification index times residue degree equals the degree of the piece. A piece with ramification index one is unramified: it refines the remainders but not the ladder of sizes.

The splitting type of a polynomial is the multiset of these pairs — ramification index and residue degree, one pair per irreducible piece, repetitions counted. Quadratic polynomials admit three splitting types: two pieces each with both invariants one ("split"); one piece with ramification index one and residue degree two ("inert"); one piece with ramification index two and residue degree one ("ramified"). Polynomials with a repeated root form a set of measure zero and never affect a density, so they may be ignored throughout.

The question: fix a degree and a splitting type; how does its density vary with the prime?

## 2. The two results, and the exact status of every part

At each degree, all but finitely many primes are tame for that degree: a prime is tame when it exceeds the degree, which forces it to be coprime to every ramification index that can occur, and wild otherwise. The classical theory, sketched in section 3, computes the densities at tame primes and shows each is the value of a rational function of the prime. The two results here concern the wild primes.

**Theorem (quadratics, unconditional).** For polynomials of degree two: the ramified splitting type has density one divided by one more than the prime, at every prime; the split and inert types each have density half of what remains. In particular at the prime two — the one wild prime for quadratics — all three densities equal one third. The formalized statement covers every prime uniformly, and its certificate rests on nothing beyond the three logical axioms built into the Lean proof assistant (propositional extensionality, choice, and quotient soundness) together with Lean's standard mathematical library.

**Theorem (any degree, conditional).** For each fixed degree, the development constructs a candidate rational function for every splitting type, by the classification-and-linear-algebra mechanism of sections 5 through 7, and proves: at any prime whose counting data satisfies a regularity condition of two stated clauses, the density of every splitting type of that degree exists and equals the value of its candidate function at that prime; the candidate functions sum to one identically; and the limit is reached in three provably equivalent readings of "density" — one keyed to the classification algorithm, one keyed to the true splitting type, and one read directly from p-adic coefficient classes. This is a machine-checked implication. What it is an implication *from* is the content of the following table, which is the paper's ground truth; nothing later in the paper claims more than this table.

Two clarifications the table compresses. First, "regularity" and the thirteen placeholders are different things: the regularity condition is a two-clause property of a single prime's counting data, fully formulated; the placeholders are separate hypothesis positions whose formulation is itself future work. Second, the conditional theorem derives the *existence* of each density from the instance data it consumes — the instance data includes the vanishing of an error envelope — so the conjecture called exhaustiveness in section 9 is not a further hypothesis of the theorem but the assertion that genuine instance data exists at every prime and degree.

## 3. The classical picture at tame primes

At a tame prime, the reduction of a polynomial modulo the prime already carries most of the factorization. Hensel's lifting lemma converts each irreducible factor of the reduction, when the reduction has no repeated factors, into an irreducible piece of the polynomial itself; counting reductions over the finite field of remainders gives formulas that are polynomial in the size of that field; and the tamely ramified pieces are governed by an explicit classification going back to the ramification theory of local fields. Assembled, these give the tame densities as values of rational functions. In the precise form relevant here — densities of splitting behavior for random p-adic points along a finite map — this is the local Chebotarev density theorem of the 2022 paper cited above, of which the polynomial-factorization statement is a special case.

At a wild prime two of these three inputs break where they are needed most. Hensel's lemma requires the reduction to separate into coprime factors, and the polynomials that dominate wild-prime behavior are exactly those whose reductions degenerate — a repeated factor modulo the prime is where ramification concentrates. And the geometric argument behind the cited theorem passes through a resolution of singularities carrying an action of the symmetric group on the roots; in residue characteristic up to the degree, that group ceases to be linearly reductive, and the construction is not available. What remains at a wild prime is digit-by-digit analysis: deciding a factorization requires tracking how carries — overflow from one digit position to the next — propagate through successive approximations, and the decision depends on finitely many digits whose number varies from polynomial to polynomial without a uniform bound. Wild densities were computed in the literature through such case analysis, degree by degree and prime by prime, and the published values displayed no stated connection to the tame formulas.

## 4. What uniformity says

The quadratic theorem makes the connection exact in the one wild case of degree two: the value at the prime two is the value of the same rational function that the tame computation produces at odd primes. The wild computation there is not exceptional; it is a further instance of one formula.

At higher degree, the conditional theorem asserts the same conclusion — density equals candidate function, at the residue-field size of the prime — at every prime passing the regularity condition. The condition's two clauses require, informally, that the prime's counting data escapes a specific degeneracy, and that two independently defined readings of one count agree; both clauses are formulated exactly in the formal text, and section 9 states their universal validity as one of the three conjectures. Nothing is asserted at a prime failing the condition.

## 5. The tree of successive approximations

The proof mechanism begins with the algorithm that decides factorization over the p-adic integers, in the line of development from Ore's Newton-polygon method through Okutsu's invariants to the types of Montes and coauthors. Given a polynomial, the algorithm reads its Newton polygon: plot, for each coefficient, the power of the prime dividing it against its position; take the lower convex boundary of the plotted points; the slopes of that boundary control the sizes of the roots. Each boundary segment contributes a residual polynomial — a polynomial over the finite field of remainders, assembled from the digits that the polygon marks as decisive — and each irreducible factor of a residual polynomial refines the approximation one level: it either decides an irreducible piece completely or prescribes a recentering after which a finer polygon is read over an enlarged finite field, the growth of the field recorded by the residual factor's degree. Every individual polynomial without repeated factors is decided after finitely many levels; how the number of levels needed distributes across the space of polynomials is exactly the exhaustiveness question of section 9.

Viewing all polynomials at once, the runs of the algorithm organize into a tree. A node is a finite stack of polygon-and-residual data; the polynomials whose run passes through the node form a set defined by conditions on finitely many digits of finitely many coefficients — a digit cylinder, in the paper's usage: a set specified by pinning a finite portion of the digit expansion. The formal development proves this identification: the set of polynomials at a node is an explicit finite union of digit cylinders, so its measure is a sum of powers of the prime's reciprocal read off the pinned digits. Randomness of the polynomial thereby becomes randomness of the descent through the tree, with transition probabilities given by counting solutions of polynomial conditions over the current finite field. This part is where the difficulty of wild primes now resides, in a bounded form: the transition data at a node depends on carries, and the development computes that dependence inside a fixed small quotient ring — the carry algebra — where the propagation of digit overflow through one level is worked out once, by explicit computation, rather than re-derived at each prime.

## 6. A finite catalogue of patterns

The step that makes a uniform theorem possible is a finiteness statement about this tree. Only a bounded packet of a node's data influences the subtree below it and the transition probabilities out of it: the current segment's slope data, the residual factor's combinatorial class, and a bounded window of carry state. Two nodes whose packets are equal have isomorphic futures. Call the packet the node's pattern. The number of possible patterns at a fixed degree is finite, with an explicit bound depending on the degree alone, proved in the development; at degree three the patterns are not merely bounded but enumerated — fifty-three occur, across all primes at that degree, and the closure of the list under every transition the algorithm can make is verified by exhaustive machine computation, which is one of the finite checks recorded as such in the table of section 2. The prime itself is not part of any pattern; it enters only through the transition probabilities, as the size of the finite field in rational expressions.

Wild primes are not exceptional in this description. Wildness means the descent can traverse particular carry-sensitive transitions — among them self-loops, in which an approximation deepens without changing its pattern — and these transitions carry rational weights like all others, computed in the carry algebra. The uniformity of the eventual theorem is inherited from the uniformity of this catalogue: the same finite list of patterns, with the same transition expressions, describes every prime at the given degree.

## 7. From the catalogue to one function, and the squeeze

For each pattern, consider the probability that a descent starting there completes a prescribed portion of the splitting type — the pieces not yet delivered by earlier levels. Conditioning on the first transition yields one linear relation per pattern among these quantities, with coefficients from the transition expressions. The system is finite because the catalogue is; summing contributions over all depths is a Neumann series, and the certified solve inverts the relevant matrix symbolically — its determinant is verified nonzero at the evaluation points where the solve is applied — expressing each density-candidate as a rational function of the residue-field size. These are the candidate functions of the conditional theorem, and their identical sum to one is proved at the level of rational functions. At degree three the full tables were derived inside the formal development and agree exactly with the independently recorded values, wild primes included.

The other half of the conditional theorem is the squeeze. At each finite reading depth, the count of polynomials decided to have the given splitting type is trapped in a certified two-sided bracket: at least the candidate value minus an error envelope, at most the candidate value, both rescaled by the count of coefficient classes at that depth. The instance data consumed by the theorem includes the vanishing of the envelope as the depth grows; from the bracket and that vanishing, the density exists and equals the candidate value, in each of the three certified readings. The envelope's vanishing is itself proved outright for quadratics — which is how the unconditional theorem closes — and at general degree is proved in a threshold form under the same instance data, with its sharpest unconditional form among the open items of section 9.

## 8. What the formalization certifies

Every mathematical claim above corresponds to a statement in the formal development, and the correspondence is the point: the boundary between proved and assumed is carried by the statements themselves, as typed hypotheses with names, rather than by prose. The table in section 2 was compiled by reading the capstone's formal statement and the certificates of its inputs, and a reader of the development can recompute it: the proof assistant prints, for any theorem, the axioms its certificate ultimately uses, and for the two capstones that printout shows exactly the three logical axioms named above.

The development also records its own corrections. During construction, roughly twenty statements that type-checked and were slated for acceptance were instead refuted by explicitly constructed counterexamples, themselves checked by the proof assistant; the corrected statements stand next to records of the refutations. Two literature imports were first formulated too broadly, caught by counterexample, and narrowed to the audited forms cited in the table. One pair of assumptions was found to be jointly contradictory — an assumed general statement alongside a checked counterexample to it — and was removed, with the surrounding material rebuilt. These records are part of the published development rather than claims this paper asks to be taken on trust.

## 9. Three conjectures

**Exhaustiveness.** The classification decides each individual repeated-factor-free polynomial at a finite level, but the density argument needs more: the mass of polynomials still undecided at a given level must vanish as the level grows, at every prime, for every degree. For quadratics this is proved. At higher degrees it is proved in threshold form from the instance data, and the unconditional statement is a conjecture, verified computationally on ranges beyond the certified cases.

**Regularity.** The two-clause regularity condition of the conditional theorem holds in every instance in which it has been evaluated, and each clause has been verified exhaustively across the finite ranges recorded in the development. That it holds at every prime and every degree is a conjecture.

**The functional equation.** The candidate functions display a palindromic symmetry: replacing the residue-field size by its reciprocal transforms each function by a fixed monomial factor determined by the splitting type. At tame primes this follows from a duality of Del Corso and Dvornicich, as used in section 5 of the local Chebotarev paper. The general statement is recorded in the development as a conjecture; no certified result depends on it.

Alongside these stand the open construction items of the table in section 2: the per-prime instance data at every degree, the eventual content of the thirteen placeholder slots, and a short list of named unproved statements inside the development, each carrying a written record of what it asserts. The development's structure makes the completion criterion mechanical: each open item is a typed statement, and its future proof would be checked by the same kernel that checked everything else.
